motor subtype progression
in Parkinson's disease

The clinical rule tells us a patient's subtype today. We ask a different question: when the recorded subtype changes, how much of that is the disease progressing — and how much is the measurement?
Parv Patel  ·  Mayank Tewatia  — Supervisor: Prof. K. R. Sahasranand
Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI)
Good morning. Our project is on motor subtype progression in Parkinson's disease, using the PPMI dataset. The one-line version: there's a standard clinical rule that labels a patient as tremor-dominant or PIGD at a given visit. That rule works on one visit at a time. What we wanted to understand is what happens across visits — because when a patient's label changes, that could be the disease progressing, or it could just be the examination coming out differently that day. Separating those two is the whole project.

One number, cut into three

In the MDS-UPDRS examination, eleven items measure tremor and five measure gait and postural difficulty. The Stebbins (2013) rule compares their averages, and two fixed thresholds turn that comparison into a label. We use this definition unchanged.
R  =  mean of 11 tremor items  ÷  mean of 5 gait / postural items
R ≥ 1.15 → TD  ·  0.90 < R < 1.15 → Indeterminate  ·  R ≤ 0.90 → PIGD

02
The subtype comes from a single number. Eleven tremor items, five gait and postural items, take the two averages, divide. Above 1.15 you're tremor-dominant, below 0.90 you're PIGD, and in between you're Indeterminate. The thing to notice is the picture at the bottom. That middle band is 0.25 units wide, but the ratio itself runs from zero to about eleven. So the band is very narrow relative to the scale — remember that, because it comes back twice. And the rule is a snapshot. It tells you where a patient is today. It has nothing to say about whether a change between two visits is real.

This slide is the heart of the motivation, so I'll spend a moment on it. A third of all the subtype changes we observe reverse immediately — the very next visit the patient is back where they were. That's not what progression looks like. Second number: if we take apparent conversions to PIGD and just add one requirement — the new subtype has to still be there at the next visit — half of them vanish. 263 becomes 127. And the reason is the third number. Within one patient, the ratio bounces around with a standard deviation of 1.22, and the band we're asking it to sit inside is 0.25 wide. It's about five times too noisy for the question we're asking it. In 73% of consecutive visits, a patient's ratio moves further than the whole width of the middle band. So if you happen to sit near a threshold, you will cross it now and then for reasons that have nothing to do with your disease. That's the problem we needed a model for.

06
We didn't jump to a complicated model. We started with the simplest thing: a Markov chain on the observed label. That model says the current label is all you need — history shouldn't matter. So we tested that directly. Take every visit where the patient is currently labelled TD, and split them by where they came from. If they arrived at TD from TD, there's an 8.6% chance they're PIGD next visit. If they arrived at TD from PIGD, it's 39.3%. Same label today, four and a half times the risk tomorrow. Chi-squared is 156, and it's not just TD — the test rejects for all three labels. So the observed label is not a complete description of the patient. Something is being remembered that the label doesn't capture. And that is precisely the pattern you get when there's a real underlying state and you're only seeing a noisy reading of it. That's what took us to the hidden Markov model — not because it was the next model in a textbook, but because the data told us the simpler one was wrong. I'll hand over to Mayank.

Weeks 5–7 · hidden Markov model with a misclassification layer
07
So here's the idea. We assume there is an underlying motor state — the top row — that moves gradually and in one direction. We never see it. What we see is the bottom row: the subtype that the examination records, which is a noisy reading of the state above it. Look at the third column. The underlying state is Indeterminate, but the examination recorded PIGD. That's the kind of thing we think is happening in the data. The model estimates two things simultaneously: how quickly the underlying state actually changes, and how reliably each state gets written down. That second part is what the earlier models had no way to express. Two practical points. Visits don't happen on a schedule, so we run the latent chain in continuous time and feed the actual gap in as an input. And one honest caveat — we impose a minimum reliability of 0.5 on the recording process, because without it the fit collapses to a degenerate solution. That's a choice we make, not something the data told us, and we checked the conclusions hold over a range of values.

Three results, and I want to be careful about what kind of statements these are — they're the model's estimates, not things we measured directly. First, the model recovers an ordering we never told it about. Going from TD to Indeterminate happens at 0.094 per year; jumping straight from TD to PIGD is sixteen times rarer. And going directly from PIGD back to TD is estimated at zero. So progression looks sequential — you pass through the middle, you don't jump. That's reassuring, because it matches clinical intuition and it was not built into the model. Second, the reliabilities. When a patient is genuinely tremor-dominant, the exam records that 94% of the time. For PIGD, 84%. For Indeterminate — 50%. Which is another way of saying what we saw in the raw data: Indeterminate is barely a measurable category. Third, and this is the one that ties it together. If you look at the recorded labels, a TD patient seems to stay TD for about three and a half years. The model says the underlying state lasts about ten. So the state is somewhere between three and seven times more persistent than the record makes it look. Most of the churning we see is the measurement, not the patient.

09
This is the strictest validation we can do on this dataset. We hold out one patient completely, refit the whole model on the other 933, and then predict that patient's visits one at a time — and to predict visit k we only let the model see visits before k. Nothing about the held-out patient is in the fit, not even indirectly. That gives 3,361 predictions and 75.4% accuracy, with a confidence interval from 73.5 to 77.3. We use a patient bootstrap for that interval, because predictions within a patient are correlated and treating them as independent would make the interval look narrower than it is. Now the honest reading of the table. The HMM is the best model, but the margin over a plain Markov chain is less than a point, and over just saying "nothing changes" it's about three points. The bigger separation is in the Brier score and log-likelihood, because those judge the whole predicted distribution rather than only the top choice — and that's what we actually care about here. And the most important caveat: what we're predicting is the next label that our own rule produces. That's a noisy record, not a verified diagnosis. So this is agreement with a record, not accuracy about disease progression. Saying that clearly is part of the result.

Once we had 75.4%, the obvious next question is whether that number means anything, so we tried to break it in two ways. First we narrowed the Indeterminate band — from 0.90-to-1.15 down to 0.91-to-1.13. Accuracy went up, to 78%. But look at the row underneath: the persistence baseline went up too, and by more. So the model's advantage over just saying "nothing changes" actually got smaller — from three points to about one. All we did was shrink the hardest class, from 441 visits to 143, and remove the cases everything was getting wrong. The number improved; the model didn't. Second, we gave the latent chain five states instead of three, to check whether three was the right choice. Accuracy was 75.1% — no better. And more importantly, only 404 of the 934 folds converged at all, and when we started the optimiser from six deliberately different places we got six different answers. So that model isn't identified by this data. The reason we're showing you two negative results is that they taught us something the positive one didn't: accuracy on its own is a misleading way to compare these models. That's why we report the gap to the baseline instead.

A second view: the continuous ratio

The proposal also specified a hierarchical Bayesian growth model on the ratio itself. We implemented it as written — not to replace the HMM, but because it answers a question the HMM cannot.
What only this model gives
A progression rate on the clinical scale, with uncertainty: the population ratio declines 0.093 per year (95% CI 0.068–0.116). From a mean baseline of 2.29 the average patient reaches the TD threshold after about 12.3 years. It also gives each patient their own slope.
Where it breaks
Its residual SD is 1.138 — 4.6× the entire Indeterminate band, so band membership was never recoverable from it. It loses to the HMM on next-visit classification in 6 of 6 splits, and a Normal distribution is wrong for a quantity that is non-negative and exactly zero at 7% of visits.
Per-patient reliability is 0.805 for where a patient starts, but only 0.050 for their slope — with a median of three visits per patient, an individual's rate of progression is essentially unrecoverable. We keep both models because they answer different questions.
Our current reading: the noise and the signal coexist at comparable size. Not that subtype progression is an artefact, and not that every recorded change is real — but that a single recorded change cannot be read as either.
Proposal pp. 13–15, implemented as specified · Gibbs sampler
11
We also built the model the original proposal specified — a hierarchical Bayesian growth model on the continuous ratio. It's worth saying why we kept it even though it loses on prediction. It gives us something the HMM simply cannot: a progression rate in units a clinician can read. The ratio falls about 0.093 per year, and from the average starting point that's roughly twelve years to cross the TD threshold. The HMM gives transition rates between categories; it can't give you that. Where it breaks is the same problem as before. Its residual spread is 1.14 against a band of 0.25 — nearly five times too wide. So it was never going to get band membership right, and indeed it loses to the HMM on every split. One number we found sobering: the reliability of an individual patient's slope is 0.05. With a median of three visits per patient, we essentially cannot estimate how fast one person is progressing. That's a limitation of the data, not of the method. So the bottom line of the mid-term is the box at the bottom. It isn't that progression is fake, and it isn't that the labels are fine. Both are true at once, at comparable size — which is exactly why you can't read a single recorded change as progression.

Where this goes next

Four directions, coming from the limitations above and from our discussion with the domain researcher.
1
Model the two subscores separately,
not their ratio
The ratio compresses two quantities into one and loses severity: 2/4 and 10/20 are both 0.5, but they are not the same patient. Modelling tremor and gait jointly should retain that, and it also recovers the 725 visits where the ratio is undefined because the PIGD score is zero.
2
Let progression rate vary
with time
Every model here assumes the rate is constant over follow-up. We want to compare early, middle and late follow-up directly. This is a hypothesis to test — we have not established it.
3
Cluster longitudinal patterns
If per-patient trajectory information can be extracted reliably, do patients fall into distinct progression patterns that might correspond to meaningful phenotypes? Not “run clustering” — the question is whether such groups exist at all.
4
Why and when does
the HMM fail?
Vary initialisation, restarts, the emission floor and the number of latent states, and compare fitted rates, emission matrices, likelihoods and held-out predictions. The five-state result on the previous slide is the reason this matters: we need to know when a fit is trustworthy, not only whether it converged.
Future work, in the order we would do it
12
Four directions. First, and this is the clearest one — the ratio throws information away. Two over four and ten over twenty are both 0.5, but those are very different patients. One is mildly affected, one is severely affected, and the ratio can't tell them apart. So we want to model the tremor score and the gait score separately. As a bonus, that recovers 725 visits where the ratio doesn't even exist, because the PIGD score is zero and you'd be dividing by zero. Second, every model we have assumes progression happens at the same rate throughout. That's almost certainly too simple. We want to look at early, middle and late follow-up separately. I want to be clear this is a hypothesis — we haven't shown it. Third, if we can get reliable per-patient trajectory information, the question is whether patients group into distinct progression patterns. The interesting question isn't running a clustering algorithm, it's whether such groups exist at all. Fourth, and this is the one we're most keen on: understanding when the model fails rather than just reporting that it works. We already have a reason to care — we tried a version with five latent states instead of three, and only 404 of 934 folds actually converged. That tells us the solution may be less stable than a single accuracy number suggests, and we want to characterise that properly.
In one sentence

Motor subtype progression is a gradual underlying process, observed through an imperfect clinical measurement.

A single observed subtype change is weak evidence that a patient has changed. A sustained one is considerably stronger. Where a result depends on counting subtype changes, it is worth checking how sensitive it is to that distinction — including our own.

To close. Our reading of the mid-term work is one sentence: subtype progression is a gradual underlying process that we only see through an imperfect measurement. The practical consequence is that a single recorded change is weak evidence on its own, and a sustained one is much stronger. And that applies to our own results too — anywhere a conclusion depends on counting subtype changes, including in the literature, it's worth checking how much it moves if you require the change to persist.
